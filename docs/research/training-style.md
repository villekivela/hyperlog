# Training style defaults: research notes

Researched 2026-11-02 for Hyperlog, an Android/Kotlin strength-training app whose coach is Claude. This note backs the default training style decided in issue #130 with sources.

**Labels used below**

- **[Documented]**: stated in the cited source.
- **[Secondary]**: from a third-party copy or summary (blogs, bookseller pages, a professor's summary of a paywalled paper). Plausible, but not checked against the primary source.
- **[Inferred]**: my own reasoning or a design recommendation. No source states it.

Primary research comes first in each section: systematic reviews and meta-analyses, then single trials. Coaches' published material is marked "coach's published material". It is practice, not research. Every DOI below was resolved against Crossref and the abstract read on PubMed, except where a bullet says otherwise. Most findings come from abstracts, not full texts.

## 1. Volume landmarks (MEV, MAV, MRV)

### 1.1 Where the terms come from
- [Documented] Coach's published material. Mike Israetel (Renaissance Periodization) defines four landmarks in weekly working sets per muscle: maintenance volume (MV), minimum effective volume (MEV), maximum adaptive volume (MAV, "the progression zone between MEV and MRV") and maximum recoverable volume (MRV, "your upper limit"). The article dates from 2017 and was updated in 2025. https://rpstrength.com/blogs/articles/training-volume-landmarks-muscle-growth
- [Documented] Same article: MV is "typically around 6 working sets per muscle group per week when training each muscle at least twice weekly", for beginners and advanced lifters alike. A working set is 30–85% of 1RM, 5–30 reps and 0–4 RIR. Only sets where the muscle is a prime mover count. Indirect volume is "factored in" by lowering the published numbers. https://rpstrength.com/blogs/articles/training-volume-landmarks-muscle-growth
- [Inferred] No peer-reviewed study measures MEV, MAV or MRV as defined. They are a coaching model built on the dose-response literature below, not findings from it.

### 1.2 Dose-response research on weekly hard sets
- [Documented] Schoenfeld, Ogborn & Krieger 2017 (J Sports Sci; 15 studies, 34 groups): each extra weekly set was associated with a 0.37% larger gain in muscle size. Higher volumes beat lower volumes by 3.9 percentage points. Across <5, 5–9 and 10+ sets per muscle a week there was only a trend (p = 0.074) toward more growth. The authors call it "a graded dose-response relationship". https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2016.1210197
- [Documented] Pelland et al. 2026 (Sports Med; 67 studies, 2,058 participants, mean age 25): hypertrophy rises with weekly volume, with diminishing returns. Strength levels off much sooner. Counting an indirect set as 0.5 fit the data best. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-025-02344-w
- [Documented] Pelland et al., 2024 preprint of the same paper, Table 2A (fractional sets per muscle a week): 4 sets is the minimum effective dose for detectable growth. 5–10 is the "higher efficiency" tier, where about 6 more sets buy another detectable step of growth. 11–18 needs about 8.5 more, 19–29 about 10.75 more and 30–42 about 12.5 more. Above 43 there is too little data. For strength, gains past about 4 sets were not consistently detectable. I did not check these tables against the published version. https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/460
- [Documented] Baz-Valle et al. 2022 (J Hum Kinet; trained men 18–35, 6 studies in the meta-analysis): moderate (12–20) and high (>20) weekly sets did not differ for quadriceps or biceps. High volume did better for triceps. The authors suggest 12–20 sets per muscle a week as "an optimum standard recommendation" for young trained men. https://doi.org/10.2478/hukin-2022-0017
- [Documented] Schoenfeld et al. 2019 (MSSE; 34 trained men, 8 weeks): 1, 3 or 5 sets per exercise, three sessions a week. Strength gains did not differ between groups. Hypertrophy favoured higher volume at three of four sites. https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000001764

### 1.3 Maintenance volume
- [Documented] Bickel, Cross & Bamman 2011 (MSSE; 70 adults, 16 weeks of training then 32 weeks of maintenance): one-third and one-ninth of the training dose both kept the hypertrophy in young adults (20–35) but not in older adults (60–75). Strength was largely kept even with no training at all. https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0b013e318207c15d
- [Documented] Spiering et al. 2021 (JSCR, narrative review): strength and size can be kept for up to 32 weeks with 1 session a week and 1 set per exercise, if relative load is kept. Older adults may need 2 sessions a week and 2–3 sets per exercise to keep size. Intensity is "the key variable". https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000003964

## 2. Steady against Rising volume across a block

- [Documented] Coach's published material. Israetel: MEV "serves as your starting point for each mesocycle", and "volume must increase throughout a mesocycle". His example rises 12, 14, 16, 18, 20 sets, then a deload week at 6. Sets are added from soreness and performance scores. At MRV the lifter deloads to MV or starts a new block at MEV. https://rpstrength.com/blogs/articles/training-volume-landmarks-muscle-growth
- [Documented] Enes, De Souza & Souza-Junior 2024 (MSSE; 31 trained men, 12 weeks, lower body twice a week): adding 4 or 6 sets every 2 weeks gave more squat 1RM than constant sets. Hypertrophy did not differ significantly (p = 0.067 and 0.076). The authors see "a possible small benefit" for higher volume that plateaus, with "limited certainty". https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000003317
- [Documented] Scarpelli et al. 2022 (JSCR; 16 trained subjects, within-subject, 8 weeks): a leg trained at 1.2 times the subject's own logged weekly sets grew more than a leg trained at a standard 22 sets a week. https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000003558
- [Documented] Aube et al. 2022 (JSCR; 35 trained lifters squatting over twice body mass, 8 weeks): 12, 18 or 24 constant weekly lower-body sets gave no difference in muscle thickness or fat-free mass. 18 sets trended toward the largest squat gain. This compares fixed doses, not a rising block. https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000003524
- [Inferred] One trial (Enes 2024) compares rising against constant sets head to head, and its hypertrophy result is not significant. Rising is a coach's model with weak trial support. Steady is at least as well supported for size.

## 3. RIR accuracy and calibration sets

- [Documented] The RIR-based RPE scale (RPE 10 = 0 RIR, RPE 9 = 1 RIR). Experienced squatters rated a 1RM higher on the scale than novices. Zourdos et al. 2016 (JSCR). https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000001049
- [Documented] Halperin et al. 2022 (Sports Med; scoping review and meta-analysis, 12 studies, 414 participants): lifters underpredicted reps to failure by 0.95 reps on average, so they stopped further from failure than they thought. Accuracy improved slightly closer to failure, in later sets, and in sets of 12 reps or fewer. It was much worse above 12 reps. Training status did not affect accuracy. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01559-x
- [Documented] Hackett et al. 2017 (JSCR; 81 adults): the error was about 1 rep when 0–5 reps were left and over 2 reps when 7–10 were left. Resistance-training experience did not affect accuracy. https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000001683
- [Documented] Refalo et al. 2024 (JSCR; 24 trained men and women, bench press at 75% 1RM): mean absolute error was 0.65 reps for 1-RIR and 3-RIR calls, with a slight underprediction. Accuracy was equivalent between the first and second session, and unrelated to years of training. https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000004653
- [Inferred] Accuracy is best close to failure, but no source shows experience improving it. Halperin, Hackett and Refalo all found no effect of training status or years. The calibration-set idea (take one safe set to true failure, then compare the logged reps with what the lifter would have predicted) follows from the 0.95-rep underprediction and the better accuracy near failure. No study tests periodic failure sets as a way to keep RIR honest.

## 4. Failure: which lifts and how often

- [Documented] Grgic et al. 2022 (J Sport Health Sci; 15 studies, young adults): no significant difference between failure and non-failure training for strength or hypertrophy. Where volume was not equated, non-failure did better for strength. In trained subjects, failure gave a small hypertrophy advantage (ES 0.15). https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jshs.2021.01.007
- [Documented] Refalo et al. 2023 (Sports Med; 15 studies): a trivial advantage for set failure over non-failure (ES 0.19) and none for momentary failure specifically (ES 0.12, not significant). The authors suggest a non-linear relationship. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-022-01784-y
- [Documented] Robinson et al. 2024 (Sports Med; exploratory meta-regressions with estimated RIR): hypertrophy increased as sets ended closer to failure. Strength gains were similar across a wide range of RIR. The authors urge caution because RIR was estimated from study descriptions. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-024-02069-2
- [Documented] Refalo et al. 2024 (J Sports Sci; 18 trained lifters, within-subject, 8 weeks, leg press and leg extension): failure and 1–2 RIR gave the same quadriceps growth (0.181 against 0.182 cm). Failure caused more acute neuromuscular fatigue. https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2024.2321021
- [Documented] Morán-Navarro et al. 2017 (Eur J Appl Physiol; 10 trained men, bench press and full squat): sets to failure slowed recovery by up to 24–48 h compared with the same volume short of failure. https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-017-3725-7
- [Inferred] How often: since failure adds little growth over 1–2 RIR (Grgic 2022, Refalo 2023, Refalo 2024) and costs recovery (Morán-Navarro 2017), the default takes only an exercise's last set to failure, and only where failing is safe. Earlier sets stop at 1–2 RIR. No study sets a weekly number of failure sets.
- [Inferred] No trial compares failure on barbell squat, bench and deadlift with failure on machines for injury risk. Keeping failure off the barbell lifts rests on two things. Failure costs more recovery on exactly those lifts (Morán-Navarro 2017). A failed barbell rep also needs a spotter, safeties or a dump, which a machine or cable does not. The starter program already applies this to barbell bench and RDL (`docs/programs/full-body-3-day.md`).

## 5. Rep ranges at matched effort

- [Documented] Schoenfeld et al. 2017 (JSCR; 21 studies, all sets to failure): low loads (≤60% 1RM) and high loads gave similar hypertrophy. 1RM strength favoured high loads. https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000002200
- [Documented] Lopez et al. 2021 (MSSE; network meta-analysis, 28 studies, 747 adults, sets to failure): hypertrophy did not differ between low (>15 RM), moderate (9–15 RM) and high (≤8 RM) loads. Strength favoured high and moderate loads over low. https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000002585
- [Documented] Schoenfeld et al. 2021 (Sports, review): proposes that hypertrophy can be achieved "across a wide spectrum of loading zones", against the classic repetition continuum. https://doi.org/10.3390/sports9020032
- [Documented] ACSM 2009 position stand: for hypertrophy, loads of 1–12 RM in a periodized fashion "with emphasis on the 6-12 RM zone". https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0b013e3181915670
- [Inferred] The research supports a wide range for size, so 5–10 for compounds and 8–15 for isolation is a practical choice, not a hypertrophy requirement. The low end of 5 keeps heavy loading for the lift goals (Lopez 2021). The top end of 15 sits slightly above the 12-rep point where Halperin 2022 found RIR calls get much worse (section 3).

## 6. Stretch-biased training and long muscle lengths

- [Documented] Wolf et al. 2023 (Int J Strength Cond; Bayesian meta-analysis): a trivial overall effect favoured full over partial ROM (SMD 0.12, 95% CI −0.02 to 0.26). A subgroup suggested a possible hypertrophy benefit for partials at long muscle lengths (SMD −0.28, CI −0.81 to 0.16, which crosses zero). The authors conclude "full or long ROM" may enhance most outcomes. Not indexed in PubMed; abstract read on the journal page. https://doi.org/10.47206/ijsc.v3i1.182
- [Documented] Pallarés et al. 2021 (Scand J Med Sci Sports; 16 studies): full ROM beat partial ROM for strength and lower-limb hypertrophy. https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.14006
- [Documented] Maeo et al. 2021 (MSSE; 20 adults, 12 weeks, within-subject): seated leg curls (hamstrings longer) grew the whole hamstrings more than prone leg curls (+14% against +9%). https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000002523
- [Documented] Maeo et al. 2023 (Eur J Sport Sci; 21 adults, 12 weeks): overhead cable elbow extensions grew the whole triceps 1.4 times more than neutral-arm extensions (+19.9% against +13.9%), with lower absolute loads. https://doi.org/10.1080/17461391.2022.2100279
- [Documented] Pedrosa et al. 2022 (Eur J Sport Sci; 45 untrained women, knee extension): partials in the lengthened range gave more regional quadriceps growth than full ROM at some sites. https://doi.org/10.1080/17461391.2021.1927199
- [Documented] Kassiano et al. 2023 (JSCR; 42 young women, calf raise): lengthened partials gave +15.2% medial gastrocnemius growth against +6.7% for full ROM. https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000004460
- [Documented] Warneke et al. 2023 (Sports Med, narrative review): stretch-mediated hypertrophy mechanisms come mostly from animal studies. Human gains from static stretching used more than 30 min per session, so resistance training "seems to be more time efficient". This is about stretching, not stretch-biased lifting. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-023-01898-x
- [Documented] In trained lifters, lengthened partials and full ROM came out similar (Wolf 2025, Gschneidner 2025). See `progression-and-partials.md` §5.1.
- [Documented] Behm et al. 2010 (CSEP position stand): unstable bases "can decrease force, power, velocity, and range of motion" and are not recommended as the primary mode for athletic conditioning. https://doi.org/10.1139/H09-128
- [Inferred] No hypertrophy trial isolates exercise stability. Preferring stable exercises follows from Behm 2010 (more load and range on a stable base) and from the practice of taking machine and cable sets close to failure (section 4).

## 7. Specialisation with maintenance volume for the rest

- [Documented] Nunes et al. 2021 (Eur J Sport Sci; 11 studies): strength gains were largest in the exercises done first in a session. Exercise order did not affect hypertrophy (ES 0.03). https://doi.org/10.1080/17461391.2020.1733672
- [Documented] Schoenfeld, Grgic & Krieger 2019 (J Sports Sci; 25 studies): with volume equated, training frequency did not meaningfully affect hypertrophy. Without equating, higher frequencies did modestly better. https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2018.1555906
- [Documented] Coach's published material. Israetel lists "Reduce volume for some muscles to focus on others" as a use of MV. https://rpstrength.com/blogs/articles/training-volume-landmarks-muscle-growth
- [Documented] Maintenance needs little volume in young adults and more in older adults (Bickel 2011, Spiering 2021; section 1.3).
- [Inferred] No trial tests the specialisation scheme itself (two muscles raised, the rest dropped). It combines three findings. More sets give more growth (section 1.2). Going first helps strength on that exercise, though not size (Nunes 2021). The rest can be held with little volume (section 1.3). "Trained more often" has no direct hypertrophy support beyond spreading the extra sets across sessions (Schoenfeld 2019).

## 8. Training after 40

- [Documented] Most of the research above studied young adults (Pelland 2026 mean age 25; Grgic 2022 young adults only; Baz-Valle 2022 ages 18–35). The ageing meta-analyses below start at 50, 65 or 75. I found no study of lifters aged 40–50 specifically.
- [Documented] Peterson et al. 2010 (Ageing Res Rev; 47 studies, adults ≥50): resistance exercise improved strength, and higher intensity was associated with greater improvement. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.arr.2010.03.004
- [Documented] Peterson, Sen & Gordon 2011 (MSSE; 49 studies, adults ≥50): mean lean mass gain of 1.1 kg. Higher-volume programs gave more lean mass. Older participants gained less. https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0b013e3181eb6265
- [Documented] Borde, Hortobágyi & Granacher 2015 (Sports Med; 25 RCTs, mean age ≥65): for strength, the largest effects were at 70–79% 1RM, two sessions a week, 2–3 sets per exercise and 7–9 reps. For muscle size, three sessions a week, 2–3 sets, 7–9 reps and 51–69% 1RM. Overall study quality was poor and the morphology findings are preliminary. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-015-0385-9
- [Documented] Grgic et al. 2020 (Sports Med; 22 RCTs, adults ≥75): training increased strength and whole-muscle size, with few adverse events. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-020-01331-7
- [Secondary] Fragala et al. 2019 (NSCA position statement, JSCR), as summarized by Len Kravitz: 2–3 days a week, 2–3 sets per major muscle group, 6–12 reps for strength in healthy older adults, and up to 70–85% 1RM for some. Training to failure has not been shown to add benefit. The full text is paywalled; I read only the abstract and this summary. https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000003230 , https://www.unm.edu/~lkravitz/Article%20folder/NSCApositionstand.html
- [Documented] Fell & Williams 2008 (J Aging Phys Act, review): older athletes commonly perceive slower recovery, and older muscle may be more easily damaged and slower to repair. Most studies did not control for activity level or use a normal training stimulus. https://doi.org/10.1123/japa.16.1.97
- [Inferred] The ageing evidence does not call for different rep ranges: Borde's 7–9 reps falls inside the 5–10 compound default. It does point toward more maintenance volume for older lifters (Bickel 2011, Spiering 2021) and caution about failure. An age band lets Claude apply both.

## 9. Training status and "Where are you now?"

- [Documented] ACSM 2009 defines training status by time: novice is no training or none for several years, intermediate about 6 months of consistent training, and advanced years of it. https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0b013e3181915670
- [Secondary] Coach's published material. Rippetoe's *Practical Programming for Strength Training*, per bookseller and reader summaries: a novice progresses from workout to workout, an intermediate week to week, and an advanced lifter over multi-week blocks. The book text was not checked. https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/20765602-practical-programming-for-strength-training
- [Documented] Untrained participants showed greater hypertrophy than trained ones (Lopez 2021, section 5).
- [Inferred] "Still learning the lifts / Progress takes weeks / Progress takes months" follows Rippetoe's rate-of-progress classification, not ACSM's years-based one. Years trained says little when training was inconsistent. Rate of progress maps onto what the coach can change: how fast loads rise and how much volume is needed.

### Caveats
- [Inferred] The default 8–14 sets is still below the volume that maximizes growth in the meta-analyses: Pelland found gains continuing, more slowly, up to 30–42 sets. High (14–20) or Custom is closer to the maximum for a lifter with the time and recovery. The starter program's 6–8 sets is its own choice, not the app's default.
- [Inferred] Set counting changes what a range means. Pelland's tiers count indirect sets as 0.5, while RP counts direct sets only and lowers its numbers to allow for indirect work, so the same program counts higher under Pelland's convention. Hyperlog counts as Pelland does (the table above), so the ranges here are read against Pelland's tiers, not RP's numbers.
- [Inferred] Most trials last 8–12 weeks and study men in their twenties. Every number here is a starting point for Claude to adjust from logged sessions, not a law.
